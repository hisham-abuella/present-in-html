Two families of variables

Categorical

  • Nominal — no order: blood type, favourite colour, country
  • Ordinal — a natural order: satisfaction (low / med / high), year of study

Numerical

  • Discrete — counts, whole numbers: siblings, goals scored
  • Continuous — measured on a scale: height, time, temperature

Watch out

Numbers aren't always numerical data. A postcode or a student ID is categorical — averaging them means nothing.

Every variable is either categorical or numerical. Categorical: nominal has no order (blood type, favourite colour, country); ordinal has a natural order (satisfaction low/medium/high, year of study). Numerical: discrete are counts of whole numbers (number of siblings, goals scored); continuous are measured on a scale (height, time, temperature). The "watch out" is the trap students fall into: a postcode or a student ID looks numerical but is categorical — you can't average them, it's meaningless. Ask the room which family each of a few examples belongs to.

Question two · what kind of data

Levels of measurement

Order?Equal intervals?True zero?Example
NominalNoNoNoeye colour
OrdinalYesNoNorace finishing position
IntervalYesYesNotemperature in °C
RatioYesYesYesweight, income, time

The levels sharpen the two families. Nominal: no order, no equal intervals, no true zero (eye colour). Ordinal: order but no equal spacing and no true zero (race finishing position). Interval: order and equal intervals but no true zero (temperature in degrees Celsius). Ratio: all three (weight, income, time). Why it matters: 20°C is not "twice as hot" as 10°C because Celsius has no true zero (it's interval), but 20 kg is twice 10 kg because weight has a true zero (it's ratio). Don't let students take ratios for an interval scale.

Collecting the data

How is the data collected?

Observation or experiment, sampling methods, and the biases that mislead.

Observation vs experiment

Observational study

Observe and measure without assigning treatments — e.g. surveying students about sleep and grades. It can show an association, but not cause and effect.

Experiment

  • A control group for comparison
  • Random assignment — groups alike apart from the treatment
  • A placebo where possible, and blinding (double-blind)
  • Only a randomised experiment shows cause and effect

The distinction that protects us from false claims. An observational study (surveying students about sleep and grades) only reveals an association — it can't prove cause. An experiment assigns treatments and compares outcomes, and a good one uses four tools: a control group for comparison, random assignment so the groups are alike except for the treatment, a placebo where possible, and blinding (double-blind: neither subjects nor researchers know who got what). Only a well-designed randomised experiment can establish cause and effect — say that as a rule students can quote.

Five ways to sample

MethodHow it worksExample
Simple randomEvery group of n has the same chancedraw 100 student IDs at random

Five sampling methods. Simple random: every group of n has the same chance of being chosen (draw 100 student IDs at random). Stratified: split into groups — strata — and take a random sample from each (25 students from each year of study). Cluster: split into groups and randomly choose whole groups (pick 5 lecture sections and survey everyone in them). Systematic: every k-th individual from a list after a random start (every 20th name on the class list). Convenience: whoever is easiest to reach — this is NOT random; avoid it (asking friends in the cafeteria). The first four are legitimate; convenience is the one to flag.

Bias: when a sample misleads

  • Selection bias — the way you choose the sample leaves out part of the population (the Digest's phone and car lists).
  • Non-response bias — the people who don't respond differ from those who do.
  • Response bias — the wording or setting pushes an answer ("Don't you agree that…?").
  • Voluntary response bias — only people with strong opinions take part (online polls, call-in shows).
  • A bigger sample does not fix bias — it only makes a biased answer look more precise.

Four biases, then the punchline. Selection bias: how the sample is chosen leaves out part of the population — the Digest's phone and car lists left out poorer voters. Non-response bias: those who don't respond differ from those who do. Response bias: the wording or setting pushes people toward an answer ("Don't you agree that…?"). Voluntary response bias: only people with strong opinions choose to take part — online polls, call-in shows. The punchline, repeated from the Digest: a bigger sample does not fix bias; it only makes a biased answer look more precise. Bias is about direction, not size.

What do the data say?

Graphs, shape, centre, spread, outliers, z-scores and correlation.

Match the graph to the data. One categorical variable: a bar chart (or a pie chart) shows counts or percentages per category. One numerical variable: a histogram shows the shape of the distribution; a box plot shows the five-number summary and outliers. Two numerical variables: a scatter plot shows the relationship — its direction, form and strength. A variable over time: a line graph shows trends. The golden rule of the next slide: always plot the data before you compute anything.

Always plot the data first

Four pictures, the same numbers

This is the reason to plot first. Anscombe's quartet, 1973: four small data sets, 11 points each, with almost the same mean of x (9), mean of y (about 7.50), correlation (about 0.816) and regression line (y = 3 + 0.5x). Yet the four scatter plots are completely different: a straight-line cloud, a curve, a line with a single outlier, and a vertical stack with one far point. Summary numbers can hide the story. Draw the two trend lines on click 2. Tell students: never trust the mean and correlation alone — always look at the plot.

Describe a distribution in four moves

The four moves

  • Shape — symmetric or skewed; one or two peaks
  • Centre — where the typical value sits
  • Spread — how much values vary
  • Unusual — outliers and gaps

Centre, three ways

  • Mean — add the values, divide by n: x̄ = Σx / n
  • Median — the middle of the ordered data
  • Mode — the most frequent value

Every description of a numerical variable covers four moves: shape (symmetric, skewed right with a long right tail like incomes, or skewed left; one peak is unimodal, two is bimodal), centre (where the typical value is), spread (how much values vary), and unusual features (outliers and gaps). We start with centre, three ways: the mean, x̄ = Σx / n; the median, the middle value of the ordered data (average the two middle values if n is even); and the mode, the most frequent value.
