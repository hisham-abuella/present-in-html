Signpost the room. Everything today answers one of these three questions. Why statistics?

Statistics is the science of collecting, describing and drawing conclusions from data — in the presence of variation and uncertainty.

This is the whole subject in one sentence, and we'll say it three ways today: now, at the section breaks, and again at the close. Hammer "variation and uncertainty" — that is exactly why statistics exists and why we need a sample and a measure of uncertainty. The two branches on the next slide are descriptive and inferential; most of this module is descriptive.

Two branches, found everywhere

Descriptive

Summarise the data we already have: tables, graphs, averages. Most of this module.

Inferential

Use a sample to draw conclusions about a larger population, with a measure of uncertainty. Later modules.

In everyday life

  • Opinion polls
  • Clinical trials
  • Sports analytics
  • Weather forecasts
  • Website A/B tests

Make the two branches concrete. Descriptive is what this module is — you summarise what you have. Inferential (later modules) uses a sample to say something about a population you can't measure fully, and always attaches a measure of uncertainty. The right-hand column shows they're everywhere: polls, medicine, sport, weather, the A/B tests behind the buttons you click. Point out that "descriptive" is the safe ground; inference is where things get subtle.

Where do the data come from?

Two stories, then population, sample, and how data are collected.

Story one · London, 1854

John Snow maps cholera

During a cholera outbreak in Soho, Snow marked each death on a street map. The deaths clustered around one water pump on Broad Street. The pump's handle was removed.

Image placeholder: John Snow's 1854 cholera map of Soho — deaths marked as X's, clustered around the Broad Street pump.

Tell the story, not the dates. In 1854, during a cholera outbreak in Soho, John Snow did something radical: he drew the data. He marked each death on a street map, and the marks clustered around a single water pump on Broad Street. When the pump's handle was removed, the outbreak eased. The point is not the medicine — it's that a simple picture of data pointed straight to the cause. (Swap the empty frame for Snow's actual map image; caption is in the slide.)

Story two · US election, 1936

The Literary Digest poll

The huge poll

  • About 10 million ballots mailed
  • About 2.4 million returned
  • Drawn from phone books & car lists
  • Predicted: Alf Landon wins

The small poll

  • George Gallup, about 50,000 people
  • A better-chosen sample
  • Predicted: F. D. Roosevelt wins
  • Roosevelt won ~61% of the vote, 46 of 48 states

The contrast that defines the whole hour. The Literary Digest mailed ~10 million ballots and got ~2.4 million back — an enormous sample — yet drew its lists from telephone directories and car registrations, which in 1936 left out poorer voters, and it predicted Alf Landon. George Gallup used a far smaller sample of about 50,000, better chosen, and correctly predicted Roosevelt, who won about 61% of the popular vote and 46 of the 48 states. The big poll lost; the small one won. Hold the numbers: 10 million vs 50,000.

A huge biased sample is worse than a small, well-chosen one.

How data are collected matters more than how much there is.

State the takeaway plainly — this is the second way we say the story. Size is not quality. The Digest's 2.4 million were drawn from sources that systematically left out part of the electorate, so more data made the error look more confident, not less. Gallup's 50,000 were better chosen and beat it. Repeat it: more data does not fix a bad sample. Everything in this section (population, sampling, bias) exists to keep us from that trap.

Population, sample, parameter, statistic

From the whole group to one number

Four ideas on one picture. Population = the whole group (all students at the university); sample = the part we actually measure (say, 200 who answered a survey). The hot dots are the few chosen for the sample. A parameter (population mean μ, population standard deviation σ) describes the population and is usually unknown; a statistic (sample mean x̄, sample standard deviation s) is computed from the sample and is our estimate of the parameter. The memory aid: P→P, S→S. Three clicks: boxes, arrows, then the numbers.

Parameter vs statistic

Parameter

A number describing the population — usually unknown. Notation: the mean μ and standard deviation σ.

Statistic

A number computed from the sample, used to estimate the parameter. Notation: the mean x̄ and standard deviation s.

Keep the notation tight. Population quantities use Greek letters: μ for the population mean, σ for the population standard deviation. Sample quantities are the Latin versions: x̄ (x-bar) for the sample mean, s for the sample standard deviation. The statistic is the estimate; the parameter is the truth we're trying to reach. Reinforce the memory aid P→P, S→S — it survives the rest of the course.
